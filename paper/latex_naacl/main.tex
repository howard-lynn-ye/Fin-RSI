\begin{abstract}
Financial agents can be given domain guidance and numerical algorithms, but access does
not establish that the agent uses either resource or improves its decisions. We present
\textsc{fin-skills}, a library of financial skills, quantitative interfaces, and executable
research checks, and evaluate optional document and algorithm access in a frozen factorial
trading study. Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 14B make \FTDecisions{} decisions across four conditions,
three sampling seeds, and two asset baskets on a shared historical calendar. All conditions
see identical adjusted prices and use the same budget, next-close execution, and costs.
Full access changes mean cumulative net return relative to the base condition by
\FTOriginalSevenDifference{} and \FTOriginalFourteenDifference{} percentage points on the
original assets, and \FTTransferSevenDifference{} and \FTTransferFourteenDifference{} points
on preselected transfer assets, for 7B and 14B respectively. Every full-access group trails
equal-weight rebalancing. There are \FTSkillReads{} successful skill-document reads and only
\FTAlgorithmDecisions{} decisions with a successful algorithm call. Calendar-block intervals
span zero for every reported effect. The experiment measures access under limited uptake;
it does not establish gains from knowledge consumption or general trading superiority.
Separate guard and code-generation audits describe the library's ability to detect specified
financial errors, without attributing those effects to the trading intervention.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A library can make financial knowledge and quantitative methods available to a trading
agent without making the agent consult them. Even when an interface is used successfully,
a higher historical return may reflect exposure choices on a particular market path.
Evaluating a financial tool library therefore requires observing the chain from access to
use to submitted decisions, alongside returns, risk, and simple strategy references.

\textsc{fin-skills} combines dated financial guidance with numerical interfaces and
executable research checks. Domain skills describe suitable libraries and failure modes;
method interfaces expose input and parameter contracts; guards check declared assumptions
such as timing, corporate actions, and validation splits. These components support different
claims. A working algorithm establishes availability, a recorded call establishes use,
and a guard result establishes only that its specified check ran.

We ask what changes when a trading agent receives skill-document access, numerical-tool
access, or both. The main experiment separates these two access interventions in a
four-cell design with identical price data. It retains every planned decision, including
failed calls and missing submissions. An original basket is already exposed to development;
a second basket changes assets within the same period and broad economic environment.
The comparison is consequently a bounded historical study of access and agent behavior.

The results vary by model, basket, and sampling seed. Full access improves three of four
group means relative to base, but falls below equal-weight rebalancing in all four. No
successful skill reads are observed, and successful algorithm calls concentrate in one
model--basket--condition group. This behavior limits the interpretation of the factorial
effects: they describe changed interfaces and prompts, not the benefits of financial
knowledge that the model demonstrably consumed. We publish complete aggregate results,
frozen protocols, source hashes, and decision receipts, keeping earlier studies separate.

Our contributions are a reusable financial skill and algorithm library; a frozen study
that distinguishes document and numerical-tool access under common data and accounting;
and an analysis that connects returns to actual uptake, failures, risk, and computational
cost. Guard detection, numerical parity, code-generation audits, and optional memory and
context services provide supporting evidence in the appendices.

\section{Related work}
FinAgent combines multimodal financial information with trading tools, reflection, and
memory \cite{zhang2024finagent}; StockBench evaluates sequential portfolio decisions
using prices, fundamentals, and news \cite{chen2025stockbench}. The Finance Agent
Benchmark evaluates financial research tasks \cite{bigeard2025finance}. Our experiment
focuses on optional access to reusable documents and numerical interfaces while holding
the available price history fixed. It does not compare complete trading systems or
establish a ranking against these benchmarks.

Behavioral testing motivates measuring concrete capabilities beyond an aggregate score
\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}. Backtest overfitting and selection bias
\cite{bailey2014deflated,bailey2017pbo}, together with look-ahead leakage in financial
agents \cite{li2025profit}, motivate frozen analysis choices and explicit exposure limits.
Our supporting guard studies examine specified financial errors; the trading study does
not vary mandatory guard enforcement. The optional memory component adapts the
mushroom-body model of \citet{huang2024memory}; no memory treatment is included here.

\section{Library and access interventions}
\label{sec:library}
\textsc{fin-skills} supplies domain and per-library skills, quantitative algorithms,
structured data adapters, and research checks. Skill documents identify task scopes,
input conventions, dependencies, and dated sources. Numerical methods cover portfolio
construction, signals, forecasting, volatility, and execution schedules. Structured
responses distinguish computed results from missing dependencies and runtime errors.
The broader architecture and interface inventory appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:library} and the supporting framework description.

For the main experiment, knowledge access (K) exposes eight predeclared skills through
paginated discovery and reading functions: portfolio optimization, covariance and risk,
corporate actions, backtest validation, backtest overfitting, data quality, signal
construction, and trend following. Numerical access (T) exposes a price-compatible menu,
method descriptions, calling contracts, and execution. T therefore includes the minimal
documentation needed to call its interfaces; K is additional domain material. The menu
order is seeded, and neither condition prescribes a strategy. Computed weights require an
explicit submission and are never adopted automatically.

Every cell has the same price readers and NumPy/pandas/SciPy execution environment.
Direct package imports are disabled, so numerical-library access occurs through the
assigned interfaces. Skill documents are optional resources, not inserted readings.
This design measures the total effect of offering each interface, including its prompt
text and discovery burden; it does not separately identify prose length or content effects.

\section{Discussion}
The main finding is a gap between resource availability and resource use. Scripted
qualification established that the selected numerical methods could execute and submit
valid weights. The agents nevertheless rarely called them and never successfully read a
skill document. A favorable return in a knowledge-access cell therefore cannot establish
that financial knowledge improved the strategy. Similarly, higher submission rates can
coexist with lower return; reaching a valid output and making a profitable allocation are
different outcomes.

The return differences are sensitive to model, basket, and sampling seed. The original
7B tools-only condition has the highest group mean on that basket, but its full-access
condition performs worse. The original 14B tools-only mean falls below base, while the
transfer full-access means improve for both model sizes. These patterns do not support a
stable ordering of the four interfaces. Common-period asset transfer adds a useful check
on asset dependence while retaining shared market direction and exposures.

Research guards answer a separate reliability question. The supporting audits test
known defects, numerical parity, and whether an enforced controller rejects erroneous
programs. Their results cannot explain the trading return differences because guard
enforcement is not a factor in this experiment. The retained code-generation study also
shows that rejecting wrong programs need not produce more correct programs. Together,
these studies motivate reporting availability, use, acceptance, and outcome separately.

\section{Conclusion}
A frozen four-condition experiment of \textsc{fin-skills} finds mixed historical return
effects and limited voluntary uptake of financial documents and algorithms. Full access
improves three group means against base but none against equal-weight rebalancing; the
reported dependence-aware intervals all span zero. The released evidence supports a
bounded account of what these models did when interfaces were offered. It does not
establish a general advantage for tool-equipped trading agents or a causal benefit from
knowledge consumption. The library's separate verification functions remain useful
capabilities whose effects must be tested with their own interventions.

\section*{Limitations}
The original assets were exposed during development. Transfer assets were selected before
this batch's outcomes were downloaded, but share the same dates and broad economic
exposures; they are not an independent market or prospective test. Model pretraining
contamination is not ruled out. Frozen present-day skill documents are not claimed to
have been available at the historical decision dates. The two models are sizes of one
family, and three sampling seeds share one market path. Neither decisions nor seeds
are treated as independently sampled markets.

The study is long-only with zero cash yield, a fixed proportional transaction cost,
and close-price execution. It does not model executable quotes, capacity, additional
slippage, or market impact. Block resampling describes sensitivity within the observed
path, not out-of-market validity. Rare tool use prevents identifying benefits among
users, and no counterfactual removal of individual calls is performed. Costs, errors,
and non-submissions remain in the analysis rather than selecting successful episodes.
The supporting synthetic guard and code-generation tasks have their own exposure and
coverage limits, reported with those studies. Human productivity and prospective trading
performance remain unmeasured.

\section*{Ethics and Reproducibility}
The experiments are simulated and place no live orders. The release includes experiment
code, the frozen protocol and analysis plan, aggregate results for every cell and seed,
and source, data, and decision hashes. Market datasets, model weights, and raw model
outputs remain on the controlled research host and are not redistributed in this release.
Readers can verify the published aggregate tables and their receipt linkage; reproducing
inference requires the recorded model revisions and separately obtained market data.
This working manuscript is not a claim of venue submission or completed authorship review.

\section{Earlier bundled-access trading study}
Before the factorial experiment, a separate development study compared a bundled library
interface against a raw-data interface on the original basket. It retained all 528
scheduled decisions, including failures and missing submissions. The bundle changed data
adapters, documentation, and numerical tools together; its contrast cannot identify the
contribution of any one component and is not pooled with the factorial cells.

The complete positive and negative results, seed-level risk and cost metrics, failure
counts, and post hoc exposure and adoption analysis are archived separately in
\texttt{evidence/20261002-trading-v5}. A Slurm memory failure occurred after one pair's
complete inference receipt had been written; CPU scoring subsequently verified those
saved decisions without new inference. The archive preserves this recovery record.
